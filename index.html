Who Gets to Be
the Expert?

In global news monitored in 2025, women accounted for 23% of expert and commentator sources but 42% of people speaking from personal experience in print, radio and television. The percentages show women’s share within each source role. The difference raises a question for anyone reading or making news: when a person is quoted, what kind of knowledge are they asked to provide? [S01]

01 / EVIDENCE

THE QUESTION BEHIND THE COUNT

A voice in the story.
But in what role?

A quotation can do several jobs. A resident may describe a lived experience; a chief executive may explain a company’s results; an analyst may interpret what those results mean beyond the company. Each contribution can be valuable. Counting the people without noting their speaking roles leaves out why readers are hearing from them.

The Global Media Monitoring Project (GMMP) coded a worldwide news snapshot on 6 May 2025. Its percentages measure women’s share within each source role, separately by medium. They do not describe how often women themselves take each role, and the six role percentages should not be added together. The monitoring is global; it is not a Hong Kong estimate. [S01]

A /

Interpret

A specialist adds context or analysis based on relevant knowledge. Affiliation still matters.

B /

Represent

A spokesperson explains an organisation’s position or affairs. A senior title alone does not change that function.

C /

Experience

A person describes what happened to them. First-hand knowledge answers a different reporting question.

Role language adapted from the GMMP methodology guide. The local exercise below focuses on speaking function and does not replicate all GMMP priority rules.

TRY THE READING TESTWhat is this source doing?+

A rail company chief executive describes its own network expansion during an earnings report. Is the contribution specialist analysis or institutional representation?

Reading: institutional representation. The speaker is explaining the company’s plans. Their expertise is real, but the function of this contribution is to speak for the organisation. A different quotation from the same person could serve a different function.

03 / OVER TIME

CHART 02 · OBSERVED ROUNDS

Two lines.
Different starting points.

The expert share in print, radio and television moved from 17% in 2005 to 23% in 2025. It was 24% in 2020. Website monitoring begins in 2015 in this series, at 21%, and reaches 28% in 2025. These are five-year monitoring rounds, not yearly measurements. A one-point movement in one series and a three-point movement in the other are descriptive changes; the published rounded figures do not establish a statistical reversal or show that the medium caused the difference. 04 / LOOK LOCALLY

SIX SELECTED RTHK REPORTS · HONG KONG, 2026

The same headline count
can hide different jobs.

These global figures are not Hong Kong estimates. Six selected RTHK business reports instead show how a reader can classify a contribution. The articles were chosen to teach the distinction, including a woman specialist and repeated sources. They were not drawn as a representative sample, and the categories below are this project’s interpretations of the published text. See method

One report quotes Hannah Jeong, a CBRE Hong Kong valuation and advisory executive, on whether a redevelopment incentive might change project economics. Her contribution interprets a wider market question. Jeny Yeung, chief executive of MTR Corporation, appears in reports explaining MTR’s results and plans. Her contribution represents the company’s own affairs. Both are presented as women; their reporting functions differ. Neither label measures the quality of their knowledge. [C02][C03][C04]

06 / ASK WHY, CAREFULLY

PUBLISHED VOICES · NOT NEW INTERVIEWS

What the articles
cannot tell us.

The published RTHK articles tell us who contributed. They do not reveal everyone a reporter contacted, who was unavailable or why a source declined. Those missing steps matter when asking why some voices become familiar. The global data describe a pattern; the six Hong Kong cases show roles; neither identifies the local selection process.

Fountaine’s interviews with 52 women academics at seven New Zealand universities raise useful questions about time, availability and familiar contacts. They cannot establish how the reporters in these Hong Kong articles chose their sources. A stronger local explanation would need interviews with reporters and potential contributors, plus a documented record of requests and responses. [S03]

07 / MAKE IT USEFUL

A FIVE-PART REPORTING CHECK

Before counting voices,
ask what each one answers.

A source log will not by itself make news representation equal. It can make a reporting decision visible enough to review. Internews’ Reflect Reality resources recommend tracking source function and thinking carefully about what can be changed in a newsroom’s sourcing practice. The five checks below turn that advice and this case exercise into a usable student-newsroom routine. [S05]

  1. 01

    Define the knowledge needed.

    Is this story asking for direct experience, an organisation’s position or interpretation of a wider issue?

  2. 02

    Record capacity and interests.

    Note whom the speaker represents, relevant affiliations and what their contribution actually says.

  3. 03

    Search beyond the familiar.

    When the reporting task allows it, look past the most recent contacts and record who was approached.

  4. 04

    Count twice.

    Track person–article appearances and distinct people separately. Keep an explicit unknown category.

  5. 05

    Publish the method.

    Show the denominator, selection rules, source links and limits beside any representation claim.

Open the one-page printable checklist ↗

An executive’s statement and a resident’s experience can both belong in a news story. The question is whether readers can see what each source knows, what that knowledge supports, and whose relevant perspective may still be missing. A headcount is a useful first signal; a record of roles, affiliations and repeated appearances turns it into a question an editor can act on.

08 / SHOW THE EVIDENCE

METHOD · DATA · REFERENCES

How this story
can be checked.

The global chart copies rounded published percentages from Figures 16–17 of the final GMMP 2025 report. Each value is the share of women within one role, one medium and one monitoring round. The report does not supply raw person counts for these plotted values, so this site does not invent counts, confidence intervals or significance tests. The 2025 website and print/radio/TV figures remain separate. [S01]

For the local exercise, one named speaking person in one article is one appearance. Direct quotations and attributed paraphrases count; a person merely mentioned does not. The six articles were selected for teaching, not sampled to estimate Hong Kong coverage. Project labels describe what each contribution does. The full GMMP rules give the subject role priority in some mixed cases, so local labels are an adaptation rather than a replication. Gender presentation is coded only when explicit in selected text or a linked selected article about the same identified person. [S02]

What we counted

48 published GMMP percentages; six RTHK reports; eight named person–article appearances; six distinct people. Four local appearances are specialist commentary and four institutional representation.

What we did not infer

No Hong Kong population rate, newsroom motive, interview that the article does not document, independently verified gender identity or causal explanation for the global pattern.

Verification still due

An independent second review of the eight local codes, course audience check and any practitioner or NGO response are proposed project activities. None is presented here as completed.
